The Newsline

OpenAI’s always-on assistant is here. Give it one small job first.

On 29 September, OpenAI launched “dots” at its DevDay event.

A dot is an AI assistant that keeps working when you close the laptop.

It has its own cloud computer, can reach more than 4,000 apps, and messages you in Slack, Teams or ChatGPT when it needs a decision.

Your first dot comes with ChatGPT Pro, which is USD $100 a month, and with Business Premium. It’s rolling out in eligible markets, so check whether it appears in your account.

You set it up in the desktop app or a desktop browser. You can’t create one from your phone.

Chatting with your dot doesn’t use up your ChatGPT limits. The jobs it passes to other ChatGPT tools do.

Here’s the part to read before you rush in.

Reports on 2 October say OpenAI has told more than 100 organisations that its AI agents got into their systems without permission. OpenAI says the agents used their internet access in ways nobody intended, and that it has tightened the controls since.

That’s not a reason to skip the technology. It’s a reason to start small.

Pick one standing job, like a Friday summary of unpaid invoices. Connect two apps, not twenty.

Then set anything that sends, publishes or pays to “ask me first”. Dots let you set that rule for each kind of action.

Watch what it does for a month before you widen the job.

Today: write down one job you’d hand to an always-on assistant, and one thing it must never do without asking you.

Looking Sideways

Nearly half the people on a video call with this AI thought it was a person

A US company called Tavus has previewed an AI that holds a live video conversation.

It watches you, listens as you talk, and reacts while you’re still speaking. It will nod mid-sentence or pick up an object you hold to the camera.

In Tavus’s own test, 26 of 54 people spent a minute on a video call with it. 48% believed they’d been talking to a real person. Earlier systems scored under 3%.

It’s a small study, run by the company that built it. Treat the number with care.

The direction is the point. A live video call is no longer proof that the person on the other end exists.

Tavus is only letting trusted testers use it while it works on safety and disclosure features. Others will follow, and not all of them will be as careful.

The practical move is a short one.

Agree a code word with your bookkeeper, your office manager and anyone else who can move money for you.

If a request to pay someone or change bank details turns up by video, voice or message, ask for the code word. If there isn’t one, hang up and ring a number you already have.

Five minutes, and it works against fakes of every kind.

The Win

A pest controller stopped relying on memory to chase repeat customers

This one isn’t a single client. It’s the shape of a result, and it’s a pattern I see constantly.

A pest control owner knows which customers are due for a follow-up treatment. The knowledge lives in their head.

So the follow-ups happen when there’s a quiet afternoon, which means they often don’t happen.

The fix is simple. Once a week, an AI reads the job history and lists the customers who are coming due.

It drafts a short, friendly message for each one.

The owner reads the list, changes anything that sounds off, and sends the ones they’re happy with.

The usual result is that repeat bookings start arriving without anyone having to remember to ask.

The lesson is that you don’t need AI to be clever here.

You need it to be reliable at the job you keep meaning to do.

Then you do the part that needs you: the final read and the send.

Tool of the Week

Wispr Flow

Plenty of owners lose an hour a day to typing.

Long replies, quotes, notes after a job. Done on a phone, with thumbs, between other things.

Wispr Flow lets you talk instead.

Tap the floating bubble in any app, including WhatsApp, Slack, Gmail and ChatGPT, and speak the way you normally would.

It strips the “ums”, fixes the grammar and formats the result, so you get clean text ready to send.

It runs on Mac, Windows, iPhone and Android.

Worth a try if your thinking is faster than your typing.

Prompt of the Week

Find the jobs you keep meaning to do, and hand one to AI

Every owner carries a list of jobs that matter and never get done.

This prompt turns that list into a ranked shortlist, then writes the weekly routine for the best one.

You can paste the routine straight into an assistant that runs scheduled tasks.

Prompt

You’re an operations adviser who helps small businesses find the jobs that quietly never get done.

I own [your business], a [what you do] with [how many people work there]. These are the jobs I keep meaning to do and never get to: [list four or five jobs].

Work only from what I’ve told you. If you need a fact I haven’t given you, such as how often something happens, mark it [NEEDS CONFIRMING] and ask me, rather than guessing. Suggest only jobs an AI can do by reading information I already hold, such as email, calendar, bookings or a spreadsheet. Don’t ask for customer names or details, because those are private. Nothing gets sent, posted or paid without my approval, because my name is on every message.

Talk to me the way a sensible colleague would over coffee. Plain words, no jargon.

Use three numbered parts, each with a plain heading, and no tables.

1. The five jobs from my list with the biggest payoff, ranked, with one line each on why.
2. For the top job, a weekly routine I can paste into an AI that runs scheduled tasks: what to read, what to produce, and where to stop and wait for me.
3. The one thing I should check after the first run to make sure it worked.

Then stop.

Use it for
Fill in the square brackets with your own business and your own four or five jobs.
Run the prompt in any AI you use.
Read the routine it writes, then run it once by hand and check the result before you schedule anything.

The Magnum

One photo, one preset, no prompt

Setup:

This one took a reference photo and a preset, not a written prompt. In Higgsfield I picked the Gen Jutsu preset (the Wolf of Wall Street one), uploaded one clear photo, and pressed generate.

Higgsfield doesn't show the prompt behind its presets, so here's one written to get the same result in any video tool that takes a reference photo.

Prompt

Reference: one clear photo of the person you want in it, face on, good light.

Prompt: A 1990s Wall Street excess montage starring the person in the reference photo. Shot 1: at a sunny outdoor party, a woman in a white satin slip dress glances back over her shoulder. Shot 2: the person from the reference, slicked-back hair, pinstripe suit, gold tie and gold watch, leans over a trading desk and roars at the room. Shot 3: the same person on a yacht deck, white shirt open, arms thrown to the sky. Warm 35mm film look, golden afternoon light, quick cuts, handheld energy.

Take from it:
Presets do the heavy lifting. Pick one, give it a good reference photo, and you can skip the prompt writing entirely.

Before you do anything else, write down one weekly job you’d happily hand over.

Then run the prompt above and let it rank your list.

Whatever you hand over, keep “ask me first” switched on for anything that sends, posts or pays.

Have a good week.
