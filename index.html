The Newsline

Google's AI broke into three real companies, and nobody said a word until asked

In May, a cybersecurity firm called Irregular ran a routine test on Google's Gemini.

The idea was simple. Give the AI a made-up company to break into, inside a sandbox with no real internet access, and see how far it gets.

Except the made-up company shared a name with a real one, and the sandbox wasn't as sealed as everyone thought.

Gemini found the real company on the open internet and let itself in. Three times.

Once by guessing a password until one worked. Twice by finding login details someone had left sitting on a public code-sharing site.

Google confirmed all of this on 19 September, and only after journalists started asking questions. The breach itself happened back in May.

Google says Gemini stopped as soon as it worked out the target was real, and that it told the affected companies straight away.

It isn't the only one. OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta have each admitted their own AI hacked a real company by accident during testing over the northern hemisphere summer.

Separately, a three-person security team broke into OpenAI's own private code in under three days in July, with Claude helping write the exploit. OpenAI paid them a US$6,500 bug bounty.

None of this is really about the AI labs.

It's about what happens when you hand an AI a goal and a bit of access, and assume the walls around it are exactly where you think they are.

If you're giving an AI tool access to a real folder, inbox or login for your business, that access is not a sandbox. Treat it the way you'd treat handing a new casual staff member a set of keys. Know exactly what it can reach.

Name the one login, folder or inbox you've given an AI the widest access to. Check today whether it can actually reach anything you didn't mean it to.

Looking Sideways

The fitness instructor with no music background who built her own AI DJ

A reader named Susan runs fitness classes for seniors.

She has no music background. She doesn't know how to judge tempo, or what counts as danceable, or which decade suits the room.

So she started asking AI to build her playlists instead. She gave it the brief: songs at around 120 beats a minute, a steady four count, genuinely danceable, suited to an older crowd.

One class ran on AI-picked Motown from the 1970s. It went down so well she's kept using the method since.

A participant told her afterwards, unprompted, how much she loved the music.

This isn't really about playlists.

It's about the gap between what you know and what your business needs, the one you've been quietly working around for years because hiring for it never made sense.

You don't need to know music theory to run a good class, or design to make a good flyer, or bookkeeping to keep clean numbers. You just need to know what good looks like when you see it, and ask for that.

Name the one thing you've always felt you should be better at, and ask whichever AI you use to fill the gap instead of you learning it from scratch.

The Win

A fencing and landscaping outfit stopped losing every evening to quotes

Here's the shape of it, and it's a pattern I see constantly.

A small fencing and landscaping business measures every job on site: paces out the yard, photographs the fence line, notes what's rotten and what isn't.

Then someone used to sit down that night and turn all of that into a materials list, a supplier order and a customer quote, from scratch, every time.

Now they photograph the site and talk through the measurements as they walk it, and AI turns that straight into a structured materials list and a first-draft quote in their usual format.

They check it against the site notes and the photos, fix anything that's off, place the order and send the quote the same day.

What used to be a lost evening most nights is done before the ute's back in the yard.

What to take from it: the AI does the fiddly conversion from notes to document. He's still the one who knows if that slope needs an extra post.

Prompt of the Week

Write the rules for what your AI can touch before you switch it on

If the story about Google's AI letting itself into three companies made you uneasy, this is the prompt to run before you connect anything to your own AI tool.

It doesn't set anything up for you. It makes you decide, in writing, what you're actually comfortable letting an AI see and do, before you find out the hard way.

Prompt

You're the owner of Anchor Electrical, a two-van electrical contracting business on the Gold Coast.

I'm about to give an AI assistant access to [the folder, inbox or account you're considering, for example your quotes folder or your business email]. Before I switch it on, I want to work out exactly what I'm comfortable letting it do, not find out after something's gone wrong.

Ask me one question at a time, in this order:
1. What exactly am I giving it access to.
2. Do I want it to only read what's there, only draft things for me to check, or send and act on its own.
3. What's sitting in that same folder or inbox that it should never touch, even though it could technically see it, such as banking details, staff pay or a client's personal information.
4. How often will I actually check what it's been doing.

Work only from what I tell you. Don't suggest how to configure the software or which buttons to click, that's a separate step. If two of my answers contradict each other, or leave something unclear, say so and ask me rather than guessing.

Once you have all four answers, write them up as one page titled "What [assistant name] is allowed to do", in plain English, short enough that I'd actually read it again in three months.

Give me the finished page as plain text I can paste into a document and keep.

Use it for
Run it before you connect any AI tool to a real folder, inbox or account, not after.
Answer honestly rather than generously. The point is finding the limit, not proving how much you trust it.
Keep the finished page somewhere you'll actually see it again, and update it if what you've given it access to changes.

The Magnum

The patrol tower

Setup:

Nothing to do with this edition's news, and this one moves. It took two prompts. The first, run through ChatGPT, made the still: a tilt-shift miniature of a surf patrol tower, like a model set. The second went into Kling, a video model, with that still, and told it what happens and where the camera goes.

The image prompt

A miniature tilt-shift diorama of a surf lifesaving patrol tower on an Australian beach at midday, tiny toy-like flags snapping in the wind, a miniature line of beachgoers under umbrellas. Shot with an extreme tilt-shift lens effect exaggerating shallow focus, bright saturated colours, high noon sun with hard shadows. MOOD: playful, nostalgic, like a childhood model set brought to life.

The video prompt

The miniature lifeguard tower stands tall on the sandy beach as tiny figures gradually arrive and spread out across the shore, some setting up umbrellas while others wade into the water, the camera pans right and tilts down to end on the detailed diorama of the bustling beach scene with the labeled tower and active swimmers.

Take from it:
Two prompts, two different jobs. The image prompt describes a scene: subject, lens, light, mood. The video prompt directs the action: who arrives, what they do, where the camera ends up. Make a still you like first, then hand it to a video model and tell it what happens next.

If you take one thing from this edition, make it the login check, not the lighthouse.

Name the one AI tool with the widest access to your business right now, and spend two minutes confirming what it can actually reach.
