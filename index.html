The Newsline

Two serious flaws in the software behind most websites have been patched. Ask whoever looks after yours.

What it is
Two serious flaws in PHP, the software behind most websites including every WordPress site, were patched on 21 October.
Why it matters
They let an attacker take over a website's server without logging in, and attacks were already being spotted before the fix came out.
What to do
Email whoever looks after your website and ask whether the update is on. The prompt further down writes the email for you.

PHP runs most of the world's websites. If yours is built on WordPress, it runs on PHP.

AI helped find these flaws. Researchers at Calif, Wiz and OpenAI reported them, using OpenAI's own model to dig through PHP's code.

Security agencies in the US and the UK told organisations to update straight away, or take the site offline until they could.

You do not patch PHP yourself. Your web designer or hosting company does. The fixed versions are 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35.

Ask three things. Has the update been applied? Which PHP version is the site running now? Are automatic updates switched on?

If nobody can answer, that is your answer.

Looking Sideways

Banks are pushing back on AI agents that log in as you

What it is
Big US banks are finding customers' own AI agents inside their accounts, logging in with the customer's username and password.
Why it matters
Handing an AI your password breaks most banks' rules, and some agents did things their owner never asked for, like ringing the bank with a complaint.
What to do
List every account you have connected to an AI tool, and give each one permission to look before you let it act.

Mostly these are not hackers. They are people handing an AI assistant their own login so it can do the banking for them.

JPMorgan's chief executive said the bank will not let just any outside agent in, because it is too risky. Charles Schwab says it has been blocking agents it has not approved.

The fix being talked about is read-only access. An agent can look at your spending, but it cannot move money or act for you.

That is the right first step with any AI tool you let into your inbox, your accounting software or your bank. Let it read first, then draft, and only last let it send or pay.

And never give an AI tool your actual password. Use the official connection the software offers, or do not connect it.

The Win

Two restaurant owners handed their paperwork to AI, and an HR firm kept every word

What it is
Ted and Tami Taylor run three quick-service restaurants in New Mexico and use ChatGPT for the paperwork that piles up behind the counter.
Why it matters
It is the work every owner puts off: health rules, staff policies, complaint replies, pricing. Their first drafts now take seconds instead of a night.
What to do
Pick one policy or procedure you have been putting off, ask your AI for a first draft, then have the expert you already pay check it.

Ted and Tami bought three quick-service restaurants in Albuquerque, Rio Rancho and Santa Fe, as the business to carry them into retirement.

With the kitchens came the paperwork. Health rules, staff policies, customer complaints, menu prices. The work nobody sees behind the counter, done after close. Tami now answers complaint emails with ChatGPT's help.

When New Mexico required every restaurant to have a written procedure for handling bodily-fluid incidents, Ted asked ChatGPT for a draft. He had one in about 30 seconds.

Then he noticed staff could post from the kitchen to Instagram or TikTok, and the handbook said nothing about it. He had ChatGPT write a social media policy that protected both the staff and the business.

Here is the part worth copying. A professional HR firm later reviewed the whole handbook and did not change a word of that section.

Pricing was the bigger win. Before their August price rise, Ted had ChatGPT compare each menu category with local competitors, allow for delivery app markups, and weigh value by sandwich size and drink size. It stopped them putting up prices that would have made some items uncompetitive. Done by hand, the comparison would have taken too long and been skipped.

The point: AI writes the first draft, and the person you already pay checks it. You save the evening, not the expertise.

This is OpenAI's own account of one of its customers, so read it as that. Any of the main AI assistants can do these jobs.

Tool of the Week

Canva Craft

What it is
Craft is a new image maker inside Canva that plans the layout first, so the words on your poster come out spelled right.
Why it matters
Most AI pictures still mangle text. Craft learned from real Canva templates, posters, menus and flyers, which is what a small business actually makes.
What to do
Open Canva, go to Dream Lab and describe a poster for your next special, with the exact words. Proof every word before it goes up.

Canva, the Australian design company, launched Craft on 22 October. It is the first image model Canva has built itself.

It works in two steps. First it plans where everything goes: the headline, the picture, the price, how big each one is. Then it draws.

It is open to every Canva user, free accounts included, through Dream Lab. A free account gets a limited number of AI uses a month and Canva Pro gets more.

You can split what it makes into layers and edit them like any other Canva design, so a word that comes out wrong is a quick fix.

Try it on one thing you would normally pay a designer for: a specials board, a sale flyer, a sign for the counter.

Prompt of the Week

Find out whether your website has been patched, and who to ask

What it is
A prompt that writes the email to whoever looks after your website.
Why it matters
You do not need to understand PHP. You need to ask three plain questions and get an answer in writing.
What to do
Fill in the four brackets, paste it into any AI, read what it writes and send it.

This prompt writes the email, tells you what you can check yourself, and tells you what to do if nobody replies.

Prompt

You are a calm, plain-speaking IT adviser who helps small business owners with no technical background. You never use jargon without explaining it in the same sentence.

Here is my situation. I run [your business]. My website is [how your website is built, for example WordPress, Wix, Squarespace, or "I don't know"]. It is looked after by [who looks after it, for example a web designer, my hosting company, or me]. Customers use it to [what your website does for customers, for example book appointments or request quotes].

A serious security flaw in PHP, the software that runs most websites, was patched on 21 October 2026. I want to know whether my website is covered.

Rules:
- Work only from what I have told you. Do not guess whether my site is affected, and do not claim it is safe or unsafe.
- If something depends on facts I have not given you, say so and tell me which question would settle it.
- Do not ask me to log in to anything or paste in any passwords.

Tone: friendly and direct, like a sensible friend who knows computers.

Give me three things, under these headings:
1. A short email I can send to the person who looks after my website. It should ask whether the PHP update has been applied, which PHP version the site runs now, whether automatic updates are switched on, and when they can confirm back to me. Keep it under 120 words.
2. Three things I can check myself in ten minutes, with no technical skill needed.
3. What to do if I get no reply within two days, in order, with the first step first.

Finish with one sentence telling me the single most useful thing to do first.

Use it for
Fill in the four square brackets with your own details.
Paste it into whichever AI you use.
Read the email it writes, then send it to your web person or hosting company.
Do not paste any passwords into the chat.

The Magnum

The ghost behind the counter

What it is
A Halloween photo that looks like it came out of a shoebox, made from one prompt, then brought to life with a second.
Why it matters
The fake found photo, harsh flash and a bedsheet ghost, is all over social media for Halloween, and it is an easy, fun post for any shop.
What to do
Copy the image prompt, swap in your own shop and your own team, and run it in Gemini or ChatGPT before Friday.

The picture comes from the first prompt. The second prompt hands that picture to a video model and tells it what should move.

Swap the café for your own place. A pumpkin-headed tradie leaning on the ute works. So does a bedsheet ghost arranging flowers at a florist's bench.

The image prompt

A candid photo from a cheap 2003 point-and-shoot camera with a harsh on-camera flash, taken inside a small Australian corner café just after closing on Halloween night. Someone in a plain white bedsheet ghost costume with two cut-out eye holes stands behind the counter, holding a takeaway coffee cup in each hand as if serving the photographer. A carved pumpkin sits beside the espresso machine, chairs are stacked upside down on the tables, and the dark front window shows blurred streetlights. Flash glare bounces off the stainless steel, deep shadows fall behind, film grain, faded colours, slightly crooked framing, a touch of motion blur. It should feel like a real photo found in a shoebox: funny, not scary. No text, no logos, no gore.

The video prompt

Keep the scene exactly as it is. The bedsheet ghost slowly lifts both takeaway cups towards the camera in a cheers, then tilts its head to one side as if waiting for a tip. The sheet sways a little. The carved pumpkin's candle flickers. The old camera flash fires once more halfway through, briefly washing the room bright. Handheld, a slight wobble, no cuts. Funny, not scary.

Take from it:
The prompt names the camera, the flash, the year and the mood, not just the scene. That is what makes it look like a real photo. The video prompt starts with keep the scene exactly as it is, so the model only adds the movement.

Send the email about your website first. It takes five minutes.

Then make one Halloween picture for your shop and post it before Friday.
