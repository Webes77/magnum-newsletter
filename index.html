The Newsline

Claude now works inside Google Docs, Sheets and Slides, and asks before it changes anything

On 6 October, Anthropic released Claude for Google Workspace.

It's an add-on that opens Claude in a sidebar beside whatever Doc, Sheet or Slides file you have open. Claude can read the file and make changes to it.

Until now, using Claude on a spreadsheet meant copying figures out, pasting them into a chat, then pasting the answer back. That step is gone.

It's in public beta on all paid Claude plans. You install it from the Google Workspace Marketplace, open a file, then go to Extensions, Claude, Open Claude.

The part worth knowing: by default it uses an "Ask before edits" mode. Every change shows up as an approval card, and nothing lands until you accept it.

There's also an "Accept all edits" mode, where it applies changes without pausing. Leave that off until you trust it on your own files.

If you're on a Team or Enterprise plan, an owner has to switch on the Google connectors first. Claude's access follows your existing Google sharing settings, so it can only see what you can see.

It's beta, so expect rough edges.

Today: pick one spreadsheet your business leans on, like a price list or a quote template. Ask Claude to look for mistakes, with Ask before edits left on. The Prompt of the Week below does exactly that. Ten minutes.

The Win

An electrician found a wrong figure hiding in a quote template

Here's the shape of a result I see constantly, told as a pattern rather than one job.

A small electrical contractor had a quote template in a spreadsheet. It had been copied and adjusted for years.

Nobody had ever checked it from top to bottom. It looked right, so everyone trusted it.

The owner asked AI to go through it and list anything that looked off, without changing a thing.

It found two problems. One total had been typed over, so it no longer updated when the lines above it changed. One line used a different formula from every line around it.

Neither was dramatic. Both had been quietly affecting quotes.

The fix took minutes. The lesson is the useful bit.

A spreadsheet that has worked for years isn't proof that it's right. It only proves nobody has looked.

Ask AI to review it. Read the list. Then decide yourself what to fix.

Tool of the Week

SynthID Detector

Sooner or later a photo lands in your inbox and you wonder whether it's real.

A supplier's product shot. A "damaged goods" photo attached to a refund request. A picture in a review.

Google has opened SynthID Detector to the public. You upload an image, video or audio clip and it looks for invisible watermarks that some AI tools leave behind.

It checks for marks from Google, OpenAI, Nvidia and Kakao. It's free, but you need to sign in with a Google, Apple or OpenAI account.

Know its limits. It can only find watermarks from the companies it covers, so a clip made with another tool can pass straight through.

A positive result is useful. A negative one doesn't prove the photo is real.

Treat it as one signal, then do what you'd do anyway. Ask for the original file, or ring the person.

Worth bookmarking now, before you need it.

Prompt of the Week

Check a spreadsheet for mistakes before you trust its totals

Most owners have one spreadsheet that quietly runs part of the business.

This prompt asks AI to go through it like a careful bookkeeper and list what looks wrong. It changes nothing, so you stay in charge of every fix.

It works in Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini. If you use Claude in Google Sheets, open the sidebar and paste it there.

Prompt

You're a careful bookkeeper who reviews spreadsheets for small businesses. You're paid to find mistakes, not to be polite.

Context: I run [your business]. This spreadsheet is used for [what the spreadsheet is used for]. The figures that matter most are [the key totals or cells]. A mistake here would cost me [what a mistake would cost you].

Constraints: Work only from what is in the spreadsheet. Report only and leave every cell unchanged, because I want to approve each fix myself. If you can't tell whether something is an error, say so and tell me what to check, because a guessed error wastes my time.

Check for:
1. Formulas that point at the wrong cells or ranges.
2. Typed-in numbers where a formula should be.
3. Formulas that differ from the rows around them.
4. Totals that don't add up to their parts.
5. Blank or text cells sitting inside number columns.

Tone: Plain and direct. No jargon.

Format: A list ordered by risk, highest first. For each issue give the sheet and cell, what's wrong, why it matters and the safest fix.

Output: Finish with one line saying whether I can trust the key totals as they stand. If you find nothing wrong, say so in one line and stop. Don't invent a problem to look useful.

Use it for
Fill in the four square brackets with your own details.
Paste the prompt into your AI, then share the spreadsheet or open it in the sidebar.
Read the list, check each item yourself, then fix only what's genuinely wrong.

The Magnum

Higgsfield Genjutsu

This edition's video is me in my glasses, with my wife Courtney walking towards me as a zombie.

The tool is called Genjutsu. It's a new section inside Higgsfield that launched in September. The name is a Japanese word for an illusion technique, which is about right.

You give it a video you already have, and it rebuilds part of it. There are two modes.

Motion Transfer keeps the movement, the camera and the timing, then builds a whole new scene around it. New person, new location, new look. Same performance.

Object Swap does the opposite. You point at one thing, a person, an outfit, a product or a background, and it changes only that. Everything else stays as filmed.

Ours is Motion Transfer: our faces, a zombie film's movement.

It's like filming one take, then sending it to wardrobe and the art department afterwards, without anyone having to come back on set.

How to use it

  1. Open Higgsfield and go to Genjutsu.
  2. Pick Motion Transfer or Object Swap.
  3. Upload your video. It takes clips between 3 and 30 seconds.
  4. Upload reference images of what you want in the shot: a character, a product, an outfit or a location.
  5. Pick a preset, or type one short line describing the change.
  6. Generate. It shows you the credit cost before you commit, and the video comes back at up to 1080p.

I made over 20 of these in a week. Most were me dropped into film scenes. Fun, but not useful.

The useful bit is this. One decent phone video of you or your product becomes several versions. A different setting, a different outfit, a different product on the bench. No reshoot and no studio day.

It won't fix a bad take. A shaky, badly lit clip gives you a shaky, badly lit zombie. Start with a clean shot.
