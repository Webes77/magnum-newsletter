The Newsline

OpenAI's new AI doesn't tell you how to do the job. It does the job on your screen.

On 3 September OpenAI released its biggest model yet, GPT-6 Astra, and the one line that matters is this: it can drive your computer instead of just talking about it.

It opens the browser, fills the form, updates the spreadsheet, clicking and typing while you watch. OpenAI's own figure is that it gets through computer work nearly twice as fast as the model before it.

The company's president closed the launch with "welcome to the AGI era", which is the sort of thing labs say. Ignore the slogan.

The plain version: the boring admin you do on a screen is now something an AI can take a real run at.

It goes to a small group of organisations first, then to paid ChatGPT accounts over the coming weeks.

When it lands on yours, hand it one small job with no money and no customer data attached. Watch it work before you trust it with anything real.

Looking Sideways

The temp who forgets everything overnight

Here's a thing I do more than I'd like to admit.

I get AI to write something the way I want, after four or five goes correcting it, and it's good. Then next week I open a new chat, same job, and explain the whole thing from scratch again.

It's like getting a temp in every morning who has forgotten everything you taught them the day before.

Every big AI now lets you save a job once and reuse it. Claude calls it a skill. ChatGPT calls it a skill too, on the work plans. Gemini calls it a Gem. Same idea: teach it the task properly one time, and it runs it the same way every time after, no re-explaining.

Nobody makes a fuss about this because it isn't a shiny new model. It's just the difference between a tool you fight with and a staff member who remembers.

One business did exactly this in August. Here's what changed.

The Win

A cleaning business stopped writing every quote from scratch

A commercial cleaning outfit, husband and wife with six casuals, handled every new enquiry the same way for years. A site comes in, they walk it, then someone sits down that night and writes the quote, the scope and the schedule from a blank page. Again.

So they taught their AI the job once. They fed it a handful of their old quotes, their pricing and the way they word things, and saved it as a reusable set of instructions.

Now the site notes go in and a first-draft quote comes back in their own wording in about two minutes. They read it, fix the odd number, send it.

What used to be a 9pm job is done before they leave the car park.

The win isn't the AI writing. It's that they stopped teaching it the same job twice. It's the most common pattern I see: take the one quote or email you write over and over, and set it up once.

Prompt of the Week

Win back the customers who quietly stopped booking

Most businesses lose customers quietly. They don't complain, they just stop booking, and you don't notice until it's a pattern.

This writes the message that brings them back: warm, specific, and with no desperate discount.

Prompt

You are the owner of Wash and Wag, a small dog grooming salon in Paddington, Brisbane.

You have a list of regular customers who used to come in every six to eight weeks and have not booked in over four months. You want to send them a short, warm message that reminds them you exist and makes it easy to rebook.

Write two versions: one for email, under 90 words, and one for text, under 40 words. Give me three subject line options for the email as well.

Keep it friendly and human, the way a local business that remembers your dog's name would sound. Mention that the warmer months are coming and coats need more attention. Offer one easy next step: reply with a day that suits and you will find them a spot that week.

Keep the price where it is, because a discount teaches people to wait for one. Leave out any hint of guilt for staying away; they owe you nothing.

Give me the messages as plain text I can paste, then stop.

Use it for
Run it as written to see the shape of a good win-back message.
Swap Wash and Wag for your own business and your own reason to get back in touch.
Send it to the customers who went quiet.

The Magnum

Lunch, with a small edible me

Setup:

Nothing to do with the news. One photo of me, one paragraph, and the image tool put a fondant version of me in a bento box among real sushi. The prompt is below, exactly as typed.

Prompt

Tiny, kawaii fondant and marzipan edible figure resting gracefully inside a segmented wooden bento box. Wearing the same outfit, decorated with tiny sweet wrappers and holding a miniature strawberry. Sitting among real sushi rolls. Macro food photography lighting.

Take from it:
Three things did the work: naming the material (fondant, marzipan), naming the setting (a bento box, real sushi), and naming the light (macro food photography). Give it a photo of yourself and the same paragraph, and you are in the box.

Before you shut this, open whichever AI you use, take the one job you keep re-explaining, and save it as a skill. Ten minutes, once.
