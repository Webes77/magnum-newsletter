The Newsline

Your customers can tell when an ad looks AI-made — and plenty of them don’t like it

A large US survey from Bentley University and Gallup has thrown up a warning worth paying attention to.

Nearly eight in ten Americans surveyed said they’d seen an advertisement in the previous month that looked like it had been made using AI.

Only 19% viewed businesses using AI in advertising positively.

49% viewed it negatively.

And younger customers were the harshest of the lot.

That doesn’t mean stop using AI.

It means there’s a difference between using AI to help make something and letting raw AI output become the finished thing your customer sees.

Use it to develop the idea. Draft the copy. Explore the visual. Speed up the work.

Then put your judgement back into it.

If your customer can immediately tell you pressed a button and sent whatever came out, that convenience can quickly become part of how they judge the business.

Draft with AI. Finish with a human.

Looking Sideways

The person with the problem can now build the fix

This week I came across a small example that says more about where AI is heading than most of the big industry announcements.

Cheyenne Dominguez had no coding experience.

She photographed 16 pieces from her wardrobe, explained what she wanted to AI, and used ChatGPT and a no-code builder called Lovable to create a working prototype that could suggest outfits from the clothes she actually owned.

It took roughly five or six prompts across two days.

That’s the interesting bit.

For years, if you wanted a small piece of software for your business — a quote calculator, booking checker, stock tracker, internal dashboard or customer tool — you usually needed somebody technical.

Which meant cost, meetings and complexity.

So most small businesses never bothered.

That barrier is disappearing.

You don’t need to go and build an app this week.

Instead, pick one fiddly job you still do manually every week and ask your AI:

“Could you build me a simple tool to make this easier?”

Give it ten minutes and see how far you get.

The Win

A care provider turned months of unwritten procedures into an afternoon’s work

A disability-support provider we work with had a problem plenty of business owners will recognise.

The owner is a registered nurse and knows exactly how the business should operate.

The problem was getting that knowledge out of her head and into properly written procedures.

Writing them always lost to more urgent work.

So instead of asking her to sit down and write policies from scratch, we changed the process.

She talks through how something should be done in plain English.

AI then turns that explanation into a structured procedure.

She reads it, corrects anything that needs correcting, and signs it off.

Documents that had been sitting on the to-do list for months can now be completed in an afternoon.

The lesson is simple:

If the knowledge is already in your head, stop staring at a blank page.

Say it out loud.

Let AI organise it.

Then use your judgement to finish it properly.

Tool of the Week

Motion

Most business owners don’t have a time problem as much as a “what should I do next?” problem.

Your meetings are in the calendar.

Your tasks are somewhere else.

Then the day gets rearranged by whoever shouts the loudest.

Motion combines the two.

You give it your meetings, tasks, deadlines and roughly how long each job takes.

It then plans those tasks into the available gaps in your calendar.

If something moves or a meeting gets added, it reshuffles the work around it.

That’s the useful part.

Instead of looking at a list of 27 things and deciding what to attack next, you open the calendar and see what you’re supposed to be doing.

Prompt of the Week

Turn three good customer reviews into a week of useful posts

You’ve probably got good reviews sitting on Google doing very little.

This prompt turns three of them into five social posts without inventing a new marketing message from scratch.

Better still, the language starts with what your actual customers already value about you.

Prompt

You're the owner of Ridgeline Cycles, a suburban bike shop in Brisbane that sells and services bikes for families, commuters, and weekend riders.

Here are three real customer reviews.

One: "Took my son's bike in for a service, had it back the same day and threw in a brake adjustment for free. Genuinely lovely people."

Two: "Talked me out of the expensive bike I came in for and into the right one for what I actually ride. Saved me six hundred dollars."

Three: "Booked a beginner's maintenance class on a whim. Best two hours I've spent, I can finally fix my own flat."

Write five short social posts for Instagram and Facebook.

Write one based on each individual review, then two that pull out a common theme across all three.

Keep every post warm, plain and down-to-earth, like the owner of the shop actually wrote it.

No hype.
No marketing clichés.
No exclamation marks.

Each post should be two or three sentences and finish with a simple next step such as booking a service, coming in for a fitting, or joining the next class.

Give them to me as plain text that I can paste, review and lightly edit before publishing.

Use it for
Swap in your own business and three genuine reviews.
Run the prompt.
Then spend a couple of minutes changing the wording until it sounds unmistakably like you.
