01 The Newsline

Your bank statement knows where the money leaks. Ask it.

Why it matters. Most owners never read their statements line by line. Claude's new US feature shows what AI finds when somebody finally does.

What to do. Download three months of statements, black out the account numbers, and ask your AI: what am I paying for that nobody has used in 60 days?

My take You don't need the American version. A downloaded statement gets you most of the answers, and you decide exactly what it sees.

Claude can now read your bank account. In America. You link your bank and card accounts, then ask plain questions: what went up, what's been charged twice, what am I still paying for.

It spots duplicate charges, fees you didn't expect and bills that crept up while nobody was looking. It can read the accounts but can't move a cent, and sign-in goes through a service called Plaid, so Claude never sees your banking password.

It's in beta for US Pro, Max and Ultra plans, US banks only, and nothing says it's coming here. But the idea works in Australia today. Every bank lets you download a statement, and that file is all your AI needs to start asking awkward questions.

02 Looking Sideways

Starbucks wants to know your coffee order before you open your mouth

Why it matters. Being remembered is why regulars come back. Starbucks is building machines to do what a good local does from memory.

What to do. Write down your ten best regulars and what they usually order or book, then greet the next one with it.

My take The camera isn't the lesson. Remembering is. Your corner cafe has done this for years with a notepad and a decent memory.

Starbucks may soon start your coffee from your face. Or your number plate. It won't say which.

On 29 October, boss Brian Niccol told investors the chain is exploring drive-thrus that recognise customers who opt in, and show them a menu built around what they usually buy. About half its US customers use a drive-thru. There's no launch date.

Nothing has been said about Australia. You don't need a camera anyway. Keep a list of your regulars and what they like, give it to your AI, and ask it to draft a short "the usual?" message for each one.

03 The Win

An insurance adviser reckons AI will halve his paperwork

One of the people I work with is an insurance adviser. He sits on calls with clients, typing into the insurer's online form while they talk and scribbling everything else on a notepad. "I've got like 15 pads over the years." Then each application costs him most of an hour of paperwork.

The data sheet, the spreadsheet, the compliance documents, all stitched into one pack by hand, after every call, week after week.

He'd already found one shortcut himself. He'd drop a filled-in document and a blank one into Claude and ask it to move the figures across. "It does a pretty good job."

So we went further. The calls are recorded. I set Claude up with his own compliance documents, gave it one real call transcript, and in about seven minutes it came back with the application questions, a report for the advice meeting, the data sheet, the spreadsheet and draft compliance documents. All from one transcript.

It isn't perfect, and it isn't meant to be. It never guesses: if something wasn't said on the call, it flags it. On the first test it listed 22 things to confirm with the client. He still types the online form himself and checks names and birthdays against the insurer's site.

His verdict after reading the first run: "Looks really good. Like we're on the right track." His estimate is that it will halve the time he spends on the paperwork for every application.

What to do. Record your next client call, with their okay. Give your AI the transcript and your blank form, and ask it to fill what it can and flag the gaps.

My take The best part isn't the minutes saved. It's an assistant that refuses to guess. In insurance, a confident wrong answer costs more than a slow right one.

04 Tool of the Week

Talk your emails out with Granola's free voice typing, in any app

Why it matters. Most of us talk faster than we type. The reply you've put off all week is only a few sentences out loud.

What to do. Switch it on in Granola's settings, hold fn, and say your next email reply instead of typing it.

My take Free with no word cap makes this an easy yes. Try it on the message you've been dreading. You'll be done before the kettle boils.

Granola, the meeting-notes app, has a new Mac trick. Hold the fn key, talk, let go, and tidy text appears wherever your cursor is: an email, a quote, a text to a supplier.

It cleans up the ums and false starts, and it only types. It never answers back or sends anything on its own.

It's free on every Granola plan with no word limit. Mac only for now, with Windows, iPhone and Android listed as coming soon, and Granola still calls it a beta. If you answer emails at night because typing them felt like a job, this is for you.

05 Prompt of the Week

Stop your AI building the wrong thing

Why it matters. Ask AI for the finished job and you spend an hour fixing it. Fixing a rough sketch takes a minute.

What to do. Paste the prompt, fill in the two square brackets, answer its questions, then read the sketch before you say go.

My take It's asking a builder for a rough drawing before he orders the timber. Cheap to change on paper, expensive to change in the frame.

Most people ask for the finished job and then spend ages fixing it. This prompt flips that.

It asks its questions first, shows a rough sketch, and only builds once you say go. Use it for a quote, a web page, a price list or a roster.

Prompt

You are a patient project partner. I need help with [the job I need done], and the result is for [who it is for].

Do not build anything yet.

First, ask me questions one at a time and wait for my answer each time. Give me two to four choices for every question, plus an option to say something else. Stop after six questions at most.

Then show me a rough sketch of the finished result in plain words, no longer than one short paragraph. Say what you would leave out to keep it simple.

Ask me whether to go ahead or change the sketch. Build the full version only after I say go.

If anything I tell you is unclear, ask me rather than guess.

Use it for
Paste it into your AI and replace the two square brackets with your own details.
Answer each question by picking a choice, then read the sketch.
Change the sketch if it is wrong. Fixing a sketch is quick. Fixing a finished job is not.
Say go when the sketch looks right.

07 The Magnum

Your shopfront, rebuilt as a tiny cardboard model

Why it matters. A tiny model of your own shop stops a thumb mid-scroll in a way another shopfront photo never will, and it costs cents.

What to do. Copy the image prompt and swap in your own shop. Then run the video prompt on the picture you get.

My take The still is the hook and the five-second video is the post. Two posts from one idea, for a few cents.

A corner shop the size of a shoebox, made with OpenAI's Images 2.6. Use the still as your Google Business Profile cover or Facebook header.

A one-line request gets you most of the still. The video is what people stop for: Seedance 2.5 brings it to life from the picture and a short motion prompt.

The image prompt

Tilt-shift macro photograph of a handmade miniature diorama of a small Australian corner shop on a wooden workbench, built from cardboard, felt, balsa wood and tiny warm LED lights glowing in the windows. A striped felt awning, a corrugated tin verandah roof, a chalkboard-style sign with no readable text, tiny potted plants made from moss, a miniature ute parked out the front with the steering wheel on the right, a tiny dog asleep on the footpath. Late afternoon golden light slanting across the bench, a craft knife and a tube of glue blurred in the foreground, very shallow depth of field, rich warm colour, sharp textures of cut card and stitched felt. No logos, no readable words, no people's faces.

The video prompt

Keep the scene exactly as it is. The camera pushes in very slowly toward the shop window. The warm LED lights in the windows flicker gently, the felt awning sways a fraction in a breeze, the tiny dog on the footpath breathes and lifts one ear, and dust motes drift through the golden light. Nothing else changes.

Take from it:
Change only the first sentence to describe your own shop. Keep the light and camera lines as they are.
