The Newsline

Frontier AI just got a lot cheaper

On 12 August, Elon Musk’s xAI released Grok 4.6. On the main independent benchmark it scores level with OpenAI’s top model and just behind Anthropic’s best, and it’s more than 60 percent cheaper than either. That makes it the cheapest AI sitting right at the front of the pack.

Here’s what that means for you: the cost of top-tier AI keeps falling, and the gap between the premium plans and the cheaper ones is shrinking fast. Before you renew an expensive subscription, run your usual job through a cheaper or free tier and see if you can honestly tell the difference. Often you won’t.

Looking Sideways

The two words that stop AI writing from sounding like AI

Ruben Hassid writes an AI newsletter to about 800,000 people, so he has a good reason to not sound like a robot. He shared the two words he types to fix it: “Use ASD-STE100.” It’s a plain-English writing standard the aviation industry wrote decades ago, so a mechanic anywhere in the world could follow a repair manual without getting lost. Every big AI already knows it.

Everyone else is fighting AI slop with paragraph-long “please write like a human” instructions. Turns out the fix is two words and a rulebook older than most of the people using it. Works the same in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok.

It won’t make your writing sound like you on its own, and it’s no magic wand. But it strips out the stiff, over-explained tone most people mean when they say something reads like AI. Add those two words to your next prompt and read the difference out loud.

The Win

A meal-prep business stopped writing welcome emails by hand

A meal-prep subscription business I work with used to write a personal welcome to every new subscriber herself. Not a template, a real note in her own voice, because that warmth is half the reason people stay. It ate a chunk of every week, and on the busy weeks it quietly slipped. We set up a simple thing in her AI that learned her voice from the welcomes she’d already written. Now every new signup gets a draft that already sounds like her. She reads it, tweaks a line, sends it in under a minute. Same warmth, a fraction of the time, and it stopped falling through the cracks.

What to take from it: if you send the same kind of message over and over, the answer isn’t a template that sounds like a template. It’s teaching the AI your voice once, so the drafts turn up sounding like you.

Tool of the Week

The image tool that actually spells the words right

Every small business needs a flyer, a sign or a sale graphic now and then, and every AI image tool has one embarrassing habit: it spells the words wrong. You ask for “20% Off Winter Sale” and get “20% Of Wintr Slae” in a lovely font. Ideogram is the one built for it. It reckons it gets the words right about 95 percent of the time, where most tools land somewhere between a third and a half, which makes it the one to reach for any time your picture has words in it: posters, menus, signage, logos, social ads.

Here’s the whole job:

  1. Go to ideogram.ai and sign in.
  2. Type what you want in plain English, including the exact words you need on it, so “a warm, modern flyer for a cafe, text reads Two Coffees for $7, Weekdays Before 9am.”
  3. Pick the version you like and download it.
  4. If a word lands wrong, tell it the correction and regenerate.

The free plan gives you about ten images a week to try it out, and paid starts at around 15 US dollars a month on annual billing. If you’ve been paying a designer for the small stuff, this one’s worth your time.

Prompt of the Week

The message that gets a quiet quote moving again

The hardest message to write is the one to a customer who went quiet after you sent them a quote. This one gets it moving again without nagging them or dropping your price, and it works in whatever AI you already use.

You’re helping me follow up on a quote that’s gone quiet. I run a landscaping business called Greenline Gardens. Twelve days ago I quoted a customer, Rachel, 8,400 dollars plus GST to redo her back garden, new turf, a paved seating area and a garden bed along the fence. On the call she was keen, said she’d talk it over with her partner, and I haven’t heard back since. Write me a short follow-up I can send by text or email. Keep it under 90 words. Sound like a real person who does good work, not a salesman working an angle. Don’t apologise for following up and don’t invent fake urgency. Pick up naturally from where we left off, make it genuinely easy for her to tell me where she’s landed even if that answer is "not right now" or "no", and give her one honest reason to reply this week, like offering to hold her a spot in my schedule before it fills. Finish with one simple question that’s easy to answer.

Use it for: any quote that’s gone cold. Swap in the customer, the job and the number, and send.

The Magnum

Same prompt, two tools, one town

A head to head. I ran this little paper-craft travel postcard of Hobart through both Gemini and ChatGPT to see how each one handles the exact same brief. Best part is the prompt is copy-paste, so swap Hobart for your own town and run it yourself.

Recognizable Hobart landmarks as tiny handcrafted models on a narrow floating landscape strip. Small details: local transportation, trees, street lamps, water, birds, clouds, and an airplane with a dotted flight path. Soft white textured paper background, gentle natural lighting, pastel colors, subtle shadows, whimsical handcrafted aesthetic. Landmarks appear made from paper, clay, wood, and miniature craft materials with realistic tiny textures and charming imperfections. Minimal, clean, airy, centered composition with ample negative space. Small elegant handwritten style title at the bottom: "Hobart Tasmania." Style: miniature diorama, handcrafted paper art, travel postcard, whimsical 3D illustration, soft pastel palette, tactile textures, macro photography feel, delicate details, cozy artistic aesthetic, high resolution, vertical 9:16.

Run in both Gemini and ChatGPT, aspect ratio 9:16

Take from it: ChatGPT takes it, because it actually knew Hobart. Salamanca’s sandstone row, Constitution Dock, the boats, kunanyi sitting behind it, all built out of torn paper. Gemini gave me a pretty toy town with a London double-decker in it and an alpine peak that isn’t our mountain. Same prompt, same words, and the difference is whether the tool knows the actual place. When your picture has to be somewhere real, that difference is the whole job.

Before you close this, pick the next customer message you have to write, add “Use ASD-STE100” to the end of your prompt, and see how much more like you it reads. Two minutes.
