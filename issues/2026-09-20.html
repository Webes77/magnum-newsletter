The Newsline

Cowork and Claude chat just became the same thing

On 16 September, Anthropic merged Claude Cowork into ordinary Claude chat.

Before this, you had to decide upfront: a normal chat for a quick question, or the separate Cowork tab for anything bigger, like a report or a folder of files.

That decision is gone. Open Claude now and it works out which kind of job you've given it, answers back straight away for a quick one, and quietly keeps working on the bigger ones in the background, including after you close your laptop.

Anthropic also added two new tools in the same update: Claude Docs and Claude Slides, its own take on a document and a slide deck, built straight into the conversation instead of a separate app.

It's rolling out to Pro and Max accounts first, on the web, the desktop app and the phone app, over the coming weeks, with nothing you need to switch on. Team and free accounts follow after that.

The rollout is staggered, so two people on the same plan might see it land on different days. The tell is simple: if your message box still shows separate Chat and Cowork buttons, you haven't got it yet.

If you'd been putting off using Cowork because it meant learning a second tool, that excuse is gone. It isn't a second tool anymore. It's just Claude.

Next time you open Claude, hand it something you'd normally have opened the separate tab for: a report, a spreadsheet, a first draft of a document. See what comes back.

Looking Sideways

The AI trick that wasn't built for you is still yours

Here's a habit worth noticing in yourself. Someone shows you a sharp use of AI and the first thought is that it's for marketing, or for coders, or for some other kind of business. Not yours.

That's the same trap as digging a hole in the wrong spot. You don't get anywhere new by digging the same hole deeper.

It's a learned habit to stop doing that, and the AI you already pay for is a decent coach for it.

Take whatever's caught your eye in your own feed lately, a video, a post, something a mate showed you, and paste it into whatever AI you use. Ask it to interview you about your own business, then adapt the idea to fit.

It doesn't need to be clever here. It just needs to ask the two or three questions that turn someone else's trick into yours.

Next time you catch yourself thinking that's not for my business, paste it in and ask anyway. Ten minutes, and you'll know either way.

The Win

A real estate agent stopped writing every listing from a blank page

Here's the shape of it, and it's a pattern I see constantly.

A small real estate agency walks through every property before it goes on the market. Rough notes, room by room, then someone sits down and turns those notes into the listing description, from scratch, trying to make a garage sound interesting again for the two hundredth time.

One agent started recording herself walking through the property instead of typing it up later, describing each room out loud as she goes, and has AI turn that straight into a listing description in the agency's usual style.

She checks it against the photos and the property in front of her, fixes anything that's overstated, then sends it to the vendor to approve before it goes live.

What used to be forty-five minutes at a desk most evenings is now a read and a send.

What to take from it: she's still the one who decides what's true and what's overstated. The AI just stopped her starting from a blank page every single time.

Prompt of the Week

Turn an angry customer email into a reply you can actually send

A complaint email is the one you leave sitting in the inbox because you don't trust yourself to reply straight away.

This prompt drafts the reply for you, working only from what the customer actually said, so it doesn't invent an excuse or a promise you never made.

You still decide what's on the table before you run it. It won't offer a refund or a redo unless you tell it that's an option.

Prompt

You're the owner of Bloom Hair Studio, a small hair salon in Southport.

A client has emailed to complain about a colour appointment that didn't turn out the way she expected. Here's her message:

[paste the client's complaint here]

Work only from what she's told you. Don't invent details about what happened in the appointment, and if something important is unclear, ask her directly instead of guessing.

I am willing to offer: [say what's actually on the table, for example a free fix appointment, a partial refund, or nothing beyond an apology and a chat]. Don't offer anything beyond that.

Keep it warm and human, the way you'd want to be spoken to if you'd paid for something and it went wrong. No stock phrases like "we take this very seriously."

Write it as an email, under 150 words.

Give me the reply as plain text I can lightly edit and send, then one line starting with NOTE: flagging anything in her complaint worth changing about how the salon runs.

Use it for
Paste in your own business, the real complaint, and what you're actually willing to offer.
Run it, then read the reply as if you were the customer receiving it.
Check the NOTE line. That's the part worth acting on even if you never send the email.

The Magnum

First light on the water

Setup:

Nothing to do with this edition's news. One real photo, one exact prompt, and the test was whether an AI could turn a real face into a photoreal Homer Simpson without it reading as a costume.

Prompt

MEDIUM: Photorealistic professional portrait photograph. Real camera, real human skin, real hair, real fabric, cinematic indoor lighting.
TASK: Use the uploaded photograph as the identity reference. Transform this same person into a believable living-human version of Homer Simpson while keeping them clearly recognisable.
PRESERVE: facial identity, age, hairstyle, facial hair, expression, pose and overall build.
TRANSFORM:
- Warm Simpsons-yellow skin, rendered as genuine human skin with visible pores, fine hair, realistic texture, natural shine and subsurface scattering.
- Large round close-set eyes with realistic sclera, corneal reflections, eyelids and small pupils.
- Short, broad, rounded Homer-style nose.
- Softer rounded face and jaw with a slightly protruding mouth/muzzle area and mild overbite.
- Strong grey-brown five-o'clock shadow around the mouth, chin and lower face, inspired by Homer's classic muzzle shadow but rendered as real human stubble.
- Slightly fuller, more Homer-like body proportions.
- Keep the subject's dark hair and hairstyle rather than making him bald.

CLOTHING: Simple white short-sleeve collared polo shirt with realistic cotton texture, paired with blue jeans.
BACKGROUND: Warm, softly blurred suburban living-room interior inspired by The Simpsons home. Muted pink wall, brown sofa, warm table lamp and a framed sailboat picture in the background. Keep everything realistic and understated rather than cartoonish.
STYLE: Approximately 60% recognisable photographed person, 40% Homer Simpson. Slightly exaggerated and uncanny, but entirely photographic. It should look like a real person who naturally has Homer Simpson-like anatomy.
LIGHTING: Soft warm key light from camera left, gentle facial shadows, subtle rim light, shallow depth of field.
CONSTRAINTS: no illustration, no cel shading, no black outlines, no costume, no mascot look, no plastic CGI skin, no text, no logos, no watermark.

Take from it:
The preserve list is doing the work here: hair, age, expression and pose all held fixed while everything else moves. Say exactly what stays the same and what changes, and even a gimmick prompt reads as a real photo rather than a filter.

If a complaint email is sitting in your inbox right now, that's the prompt to run first.
