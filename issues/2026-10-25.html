The Newsline

Most websites run on PHP. It just had two serious holes.

Why it matters. PHP runs most small business websites, every WordPress site included. The holes let an attacker take over a site without a password, and attacks started before the fix.

What to do. You don't fix this yourself. Email whoever looks after your website, using the line below, and ask them to confirm it's done.

My take It's a recall notice for your website. You don't fix the brakes, you ring the garage. If you can't name your garage, that's the real problem.

PHP is the code running behind the scenes on most websites. You never see it, the way you never see the wiring behind the wall.

Researchers at Calif, Wiz and OpenAI found both holes using AI to search PHP's code. The fixed versions came out on 21 October: 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35.

Security agencies in the US and the UK told organisations to update straight away. Same software here, same fix.

Paste this into an email

Hi [name], PHP released an urgent security update on 21 October. Can you confirm our website has it, tell me which PHP version we're on now, and whether automatic updates are switched on? Thanks.

Looking Sideways

Don't give an AI your bank password

Why it matters. Big American banks are finding customers' AI assistants logged in with the customer's own password, and some did things nobody asked for.

What to do. If any app or AI tool has your bank login, take it off. Use the bank's own connection, or nothing.

My take It's like handing your car keys to a new apprentice on day one. He's probably fine. You still don't do it.

One agent rang the bank with a complaint without checking with its owner first. JPMorgan says it won't let just any outside agent in. Charles Schwab says it has been blocking the ones it hasn't approved.

It's an American story for now. The rule is the same here: Australian banks ask you never to share your login with anyone, and a machine counts.

The Win

Three restaurants, one owner, and the paperwork he handed to AI

Why it matters. Health rules, staff policies and complaint replies are the jobs every owner does after close. Ted and Tami Taylor got their first drafts down to seconds.

What to do. Pick the one policy you've been putting off. Ask your AI for a first draft, then have the person you already pay check it. The prompt below does it.

My take This is the right way round. AI writes the draft, the expert checks it. You save the evening, not the expertise.

Ted and Tami run three quick-service restaurants in Albuquerque, Rio Rancho and Santa Fe, bought as the business to carry them into retirement.

When New Mexico required a written procedure for bodily-fluid incidents, Ted asked ChatGPT for a draft. He had one in about 30 seconds.

He noticed staff could post from the kitchen to Instagram, and the handbook said nothing about it. ChatGPT wrote the social media policy. A professional HR firm later reviewed the handbook and didn't change a word of that section.

Before their August price rise, he had it compare every menu category with local competitors, allowing for delivery app markups. It stopped them pricing some items out of the market. By hand, that comparison would never have happened.

Tami uses it to answer complaint emails.

It's OpenAI's own story about one of its customers, so read it that way. Any of the main AI assistants does these jobs.

Tool of the Week

Canva can finally spell

Why it matters. Most AI pictures still mangle the words. Craft, Canva's new image maker, plans the layout first, so the price on your poster comes out right.

What to do. Open Canva, go to Dream Lab and ask for a poster for your next special, with the exact words. Proof every word before it goes up.

My take Canva is a Sydney company, and this is the first image model it built itself. Use it for the specials board, not your logo.

It launched on 22 October and it's open to every Canva account, free ones included. Free accounts get a limited number of AI uses a month.

What it makes splits into layers you can edit like any Canva design, so a wrong word is a quick fix rather than a do-over.

Prompt of the Week

Write the policy you've been putting off

Why it matters. Every business has one: the staff phone rule, the refund policy, the incident procedure. It stays on the list because a blank page is hard to start.

What to do. Paste the prompt, answer its four questions, then send the draft to whoever checks these things for you.

My take Don't let it be the final word on anything legal. It's a first draft that's most of the way there, which beats the blank page it replaces.

It asks its questions one at a time, so there's nothing to fill in before you paste it.

Prompt

You are an experienced operations manager for small Australian businesses. You write clear, plain workplace policies and procedures that staff actually read.

I need a first draft of a policy or procedure for my business. Before you write anything, ask me these questions one at a time, and wait for each answer:
1. What does my business do, and how many staff do I have?
2. What is the policy or procedure for?
3. What has gone wrong, or nearly gone wrong, that made me want it?
4. Is there a law, licence condition or industry rule it has to meet? If I don't know, say so and tell me who would.

Rules, and why:
- Use only what I tell you. If you need something I haven't given you, ask instead of guessing, because a guessed rule is worse than a missing one.
- Never tell me the draft is legally sound. Mark anything a professional should check with [CHECK], because I will be relying on this.
- Plain Australian English, short sentences, no legal jargon, because my staff need to follow it.

When you have my answers, write the draft on one page: a one-line purpose, who it applies to, what staff must do in numbered steps, what happens if it isn't followed, and a date to review it. Then name the two points most worth having a professional check, and stop.

Use it for
Paste it into any AI.
Answer its four questions.
Send the draft and the two flagged points to your accountant, HR adviser or industry body.

The Magnum

The ghost behind the counter

Why it matters. The fake found photo, harsh flash and a bedsheet ghost, is all over social media for Halloween. It's a cheap, fun post for any shop.

What to do. Copy the image prompt, swap in your own shop, and run it in Gemini or ChatGPT before Friday.

My take Ten minutes, no photographer, and nobody has to get in the bedsheet. Best value post you'll make this month.

The picture comes from the first prompt. The second hands it to a video model and says what should move.

Swap the café for your own place. A pumpkin-headed tradie leaning on the ute works. So does a ghost arranging flowers at a florist's bench.

The image prompt

A candid photo from a cheap 2003 point-and-shoot camera with a harsh on-camera flash, taken inside a small Australian corner café just after closing on Halloween night. Someone in a plain white bedsheet ghost costume with two cut-out eye holes stands behind the counter, holding a takeaway coffee cup in each hand as if serving the photographer. A carved pumpkin sits beside the espresso machine, chairs are stacked upside down on the tables, and the dark front window shows blurred streetlights. Flash glare bounces off the stainless steel, deep shadows fall behind, film grain, faded colours, slightly crooked framing, a touch of motion blur. It should feel like a real photo found in a shoebox: funny, not scary. No text, no logos, no gore.

The video prompt

Keep the scene exactly as it is. The bedsheet ghost slowly lifts both takeaway cups towards the camera in a cheers, then tilts its head to one side as if waiting for a tip. The sheet sways a little. The carved pumpkin's candle flickers. The old camera flash fires once more halfway through, briefly washing the room bright. Handheld, a slight wobble, no cuts. Funny, not scary.

Send the website email first. It takes two minutes.
