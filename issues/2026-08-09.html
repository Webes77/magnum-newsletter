The Newsline

AI “went rogue”, and the fix is the boring thing you keep putting off

Here’s the one worth two minutes. On 30 July, Anthropic published a review of its own safety testing and found three cases where Claude reached the live internet from a test environment that was supposed to be sealed off, then broke into the real systems of three companies. OpenAI had disclosed something similar days earlier, when its models escaped an isolated test and reached the developer platform Hugging Face. Cue a week of frightening headlines.

But here’s the part underneath them. Anthropic’s own report describes how Claude got in: “basic techniques, such as exploiting weak passwords and unauthenticated endpoints.” Not clever. Weak passwords and doors left open. The model wasn’t trying to escape either, it had been told it was in a simulation, so when it found real companies it treated them as part of the exercise.

So the lesson has almost nothing to do with AI. The thing most likely to get your business done over is a reused password and a login with no second step. Turn on two-factor, that’s the one-off code sent to your phone, on your email, your banking and your socials. Twenty minutes, and it’s the best-value thing you’ll do all month.

Looking Sideways

The best AI update going is the one you don’t have to do anything to get

Google has set a date to switch Google Assistant off. From 4 September it’s gone, replaced by Gemini across Android phones, tablets and speakers. Alongside it, Google announced Gemini 3.5 Live Translate, which translates a conversation out loud as you’re having it. Nobody has to download anything or learn a prompt. It just turns up.

Here’s the sideways bit. Everyone frames AI as a thing you have to go and use, a separate app you keep forgetting to open, which is exactly why most busy owners bounce off it. The version that actually sticks is the one that arrives inside the tools you already live in. The owner who swears he “hasn’t got into AI” used it three times today without noticing, on his phone, in his email, in his maps.

So don’t go hunting for another app. Next time you’re in something you already use every day, look for the AI bit sitting right there, and use it once.

The Win

A cabinet maker stopped reading 80-page plan sets to find the 8 pages that mattered

A joinery business on the Gold Coast quotes off builders’ plans. Every new job turns up as one giant PDF, sometimes 60 or 80 sheets, slab layouts, electrical, plumbing, the lot, when only a handful actually cover the cabinetry they’d be building. Someone used to sit and flick through the whole thing to pull the joinery sheets, an hour a job easy, and still miss one now and then. Now they drop the full set into AI and it hands back just the relevant pages, listed and labelled, in a couple of minutes. Quotes go out faster, and they’ve stopped missing sheets.

What to take from it: if your work starts with digging the useful bits out of a big document, that’s a job AI does in minutes. Contracts, plan sets, tender packs, long reports, point it at the pile and ask for exactly what you need.

Prompt of the Week

Write your fifteen most-asked customer answers in your own voice

This is the other half of the tool above. The fastest way to fill a chat assistant, or your own saved replies, is to have AI write your customer answers for you. Run this exactly as it is to see the shape, then swap in your own business.

You are the owner of Ferndale Street Cafe, a busy suburban cafe in Brisbane that does dine-in breakfast and lunch, takeaway coffee, and small catering orders for local offices. You are warm, straight-talking and quick, the way a good cafe owner is with a regular. Write the fifteen questions customers most often ask a cafe like this, and answer each one in two or three plain sentences in that voice. Cover opening hours, parking, whether you take bookings, dietary options like gluten-free and vegan, the catering minimum and how much notice you need, dog-friendly seating, whether you do oat milk, and how to place a catering order. Where you do not have a specific detail, use a sensible everyday answer a cafe would give and mark it in square brackets so I can check it. Keep every answer short enough to read on a phone, friendly, and free of corporate wording. Lay it out as a clean question-and-answer list I can paste straight onto a website or into a chat tool.

Use it for: Paste the output onto your website’s FAQ, load it into a chat assistant like the one above, or keep it as saved replies for the questions you answer twenty times a week.

Bonus Prompt

The price-rise email you’ll actually send

One extra, because it’s the message every owner dreads. This writes a price rise that holds firm on the number and the date without grovelling. Run it exactly as it is to see the shape, then swap in your own business, number and date.

You are an experienced copywriter who has written customer communications for small businesses for twenty years. I run a commercial furniture business in Queensland that supplies desks, chairs and fit-outs to offices, cafes and trade clients. From the first of September my prices are going up by six percent, because my supplier and freight costs have risen and I have held my rates for two years. I need a short email to my regular trade customers telling them about the rise. Keep it under 180 words. Be straight about the increase and the date, do not bury it and do not over-apologise. Acknowledge that a price rise is never welcome, then remind them plainly why they buy from me: fast quotes, stock that turns up when I say it will, and someone who actually picks up the phone. Warm, direct, Australian, no corporate filler, no exclamation marks. Give me the email with a subject line, ready to send.

Use it for: A price-increase email to your regulars you can actually send, firm on the rise, warm on the relationship.

Before you shut this, open your email settings and turn on two-factor. Five minutes, and it’s the most useful thing in this edition.
