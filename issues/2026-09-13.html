The Newsline

Gemini planned a hike, and nobody checked its work

On 29 August, three men from Roseville, California asked Google's Gemini to plan a climb up Mount Shasta.

Gemini picked the Clear Creek Route, told them to expect about an eight hour climb, and told them how much food and water to pack.

They set off before dawn on the Sunday and reached the summit at seven that evening, eleven hours past Gemini's estimate, with a fraction of the food and water the day actually needed.

Coming down in the dark they lost the route and ended up in Mud Creek Canyon. One man fell and hurt his knee.

US Forest Service rangers and the Siskiyou County Sheriff's Office found the group the next morning and walked them out.

None of this is really about hiking.

AI is very good at sounding certain even when it's wrong, and it will hand you a confident number for almost anything: a trade timeframe, a compliance answer, a delivery date, a quote.

Before you act on one that actually costs you something if it's wrong, check it against a person or a source who'd know.

Pick the one thing you're most likely to ask AI to plan or estimate for you, and name the one place you'll check it against before you commit to it.

Looking Sideways

AI works best where a person's attention runs out

In Taiwan, an AI reads every patient's ECG the moment it's taken, across two hospitals, and flags whoever is at high risk of dying within 90 days. It doesn't diagnose or treat anyone, it just makes a doctor look twice.

Among the patients it flagged as high risk, deaths dropped from 23% to 16% once doctors got the alert. Roughly one life saved for every 14 people it flagged.

In Nigeria, high school students spent six weeks after school working through English grammar and writing with Microsoft Copilot, a teacher in the room the whole time. Researchers estimate they gained 1.5 to two years of typical learning progress.

Neither of these is AI doing the job. It's AI sitting exactly where a doctor's or a teacher's attention runs out, with a person still doing the actual work.

The question worth asking about your own business isn't whether AI can do your job. It's where your attention runs out, and what you'd catch if something else was watching there too.

Name the one task you're most likely to drop because you're juggling everything else, and think about what could be watching it instead.

The Win

A panel beater stopped writing the same report twice

Here's the shape of it, and it's a pattern I see constantly.

A panel beater, small shop, does every assessment himself: photos, a walk around the car, notes on what's damaged and what it'll take to fix.

Then he'd sit down at night and turn those same notes into the insurer's report, in their format, before the job could even start.

He started dictating the walk around into his phone on the spot and having AI turn it straight into the insurer's report, panel by panel.

He checks it against the photos and the car in front of him, fixes anything wrong, and sends it. The report that used to eat his evening is done before the car's even in the bay.

What to take from it: the AI drafts the report. He's still the one who knows if it's actually right, and he checks every line before it goes out.

Prompt of the Week

Turn a client walk-through into a job list and a follow-up email

After a site visit or a phone call, the notes are the easy part. Turning them into a job list and a client email without inventing anything you didn't actually agree to is where it usually goes wrong.

This prompt does both in one pass, and it's built to flag what's unclear instead of guessing at it.

Prompt

You're the owner of Elevate Landscaping, a garden maintenance and landscaping business on the Gold Coast.

Here are my rough notes from a walk-through with a client this morning: [paste your notes or voice-to-text transcript here]

Turn these into two things: a job list for our records, and a follow-up email to the client confirming what we discussed.

For the job list, group the work into what we can do on the next visit and what needs a separate quote. Note if any job depends on something else being done first.

For the client email, thank them for their time, confirm what we agreed to do and when, and ask about anything that was left unclear in the notes. Don't guess at a price, a date or a scope we didn't actually discuss, mark it as [TO CONFIRM] instead so we check it rather than assume it.

Keep the email under 150 words, warm and plain, the way a local business owner would write it, not a corporate one.

Give me the job list first as a short bullet list, then the email as plain text I can paste, review and send.

Use it for
Swap in your own notes from today's visit or call.
Run it straight after, while the details are fresh.
Anything marked [TO CONFIRM] is your cue to check, not skip.
